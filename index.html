How the numbers are made

All properties come from reference data in the NIST Chemistry WebBook, which evaluates the Gao et al. (2023) reference equation of state for ammonia [1, 2]. I fitted three compact models to more than 300 NIST points (0.06 to 105 bar, −78 to 450 °C) so the page runs offline:

  1. Vapour pressure: a Wagner-type equation [3] with Tc = 405.56 K and pc = 113.634 bar. Largest deviation from NIST: 0.05 %.
  2. Saturated liquid and vapour h, s and ρ: ancillary power series in (1 − T/Tc). Largest deviation: 0.8 kJ/kg in h, 0.2 % in liquid density.
  3. Superheated vapour: ideal-gas part with Planck–Einstein terms [4] plus a residual Gibbs-energy series in p/pc and Tc/T, fitted to NIST h, s and ρ up to 80 bar. RMS deviation 0.15 kJ/kg, largest 1.6 kJ/kg. With one whole isobar left out of the fit (10, 20, 40 or 60 bar), the largest error on that isobar stays below 1.3 kJ/kg.

Cycle assumptions follow the standard vapour-compression analysis [5, 6]: no pressure drop in pipes or heat exchangers, adiabatic compressor with isentropic efficiency ηis = (h2s − h1)/(h2 − h1), isenthalpic throttling h4 = h3, and subcooled liquid taken as h ≈ hf(T). COPH = (h2 − h3)/(h2 − h1). The Carnot COP uses the condensing and evaporating temperatures, Tcond/(Tcond − Tevap).

Phase diagram: the vaporisation curve is the Wagner equation. The sublimation curve is the Clausius–Clapeyron equation through the triple point (195.49 K, 0.0605 bar) with Δhsub = 31.2 kJ/mol measured by Overstreet and Giauque [7]. The melting curve is drawn schematically: it rises very steeply from the triple point, and no fitted melting equation is used here.
